Reality is an entity of vast, irreducible complexity. It is far more than the human mind can grasp, yet we are forced to operate within it. To cope, we rely on simplified models and simulations; essentially, shorthand versions of the world that fit inside our heads. The problem is fidelity. Eventually, the model breaks, and we are forced to confront phenomena we didn't account for and don't know how to handle.

To eliminate these 'edge cases,' humanity has spent millennia on a grand project: forcing reality to conform to a model we can predict and control. Philosophers have both fueled this project and warned of its side effects, warnings we have summarily ignored. It may seem like a non sequitur, but the culmination of this effort is the 'AI Slop' currently inundating us. An ironic final step in subverting our perception of reality itself.

## The legibility project of the ancients

Humans tend to be "illegible". They are complicated, and diverse. Every group has its own customs, traditions and morality. Controlling and ruling over such an illegible group is near impossible. So this grand project started millennia ago as a mechanism to control people by reducing their illegibility. By making them legible. By flattening their complexity and diversity into a 'compressible' set of behaviors. The first recorded efforts in this direction are the [Code of Hammurabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Code_of_Hammurabi) and the [Manusmṛti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manusmriti). Yes, these are known to be the first legal texts, but then again, what is a legal system beyond conforming a diverse population to predictable less-diverse set of behaviors; a simple model of human behavior! Of course, there were errors in predictions of these models, and such errors are referred to as "crimes" and there are entire institutions dedicated to "correcting" such errors, by not improving the model, but by coercing human behaviors to fit the model. "Justice" was really about systemic control. 

Of course, due to limitations of technology, the model had incredibly low resolution, and it sought to model only the human behaviors the needed control within the confines of the day's technology and political sovereignty. For the most part, these models left the natural world and our inner worlds alone. 

However, the philosophers of this time were active in laying the groundwork for what was to come next: attempting to coerce the natural world into legible models. Plato postulated his [Theory of forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_forms), which modeled the diversity we see in the world as a collection of "noise" deviating from the ideal. Reality was the ideal, which implicitly gave permission to treat the "noise" as something to be eliminated. Aristotle divided the natural world and phenomena into distinct categories, which ushered in the methodology on deconstructing the whole into pieces with a tacit assumption that nothing could be lost in between. He created "silos of legibility", which sanctioned further flattening of the natural world with ever more complex models, with ever less explicable noise.  [Aryabhata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhata) and [Brahmagupta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmagupta) created abstract models of the world and the heavens in their mathematics. Crucially, these ancient models remained descriptive--they were (imperfect) maps used to navigate a complex world. The shift to a prescriptive reality, where the world was forced to conform to the map, was the work of the Renaissance and the Industrial Revolution.

## The objectivity of modernity 
The renaissance and modernity brought sweeping changes to humanity’s view of the natural world. Gone were the prisms of religion and divinity that gave our reality a teleology and the perception of the world as a whole. The new episteme was that of science and rationality. 

Dusting off the Aristotelian categories, the philosophers of this age charted a new path in understanding our natural world. Our understanding of the world was now divided into neat categories that deserved deeper and focused study. And the investigations into these categories could happen concurrently and independently with the assumption that three things are true: there is a truth of the matter, that truth is discoverable by humanity, and rationality is the tool by which that truth can be discovered. We had entered the era of objectivity. 

The world and reality was an object to be observed, dissected, studied. And somehow, we could do it objectively, as if we weren’t part of the world and reality at all. Suddenly, we were alienated from our reality, and with that, from our own existence. 

This alienation allowed philosophers to turn this gaze of objectivity inward into our own lives and how we relate to each other; into our inter subjectivity. They dissected how we relate to each other, and how we work tougher to produce goods and make progress. This was categorized and studied with ever more precision. We had new categories to peer into. There was economics and there was psychology and there was political science and there was ethics. Almost as if each of them had nothing to do with each other, and pursued their own investigations to get to their objective truths. 

It wasn’t long before this inward gaze of economics suddenly declared that a pre-modern profession was really a collection of distinct tasks that could be studied, pursued, and practiced on its own. A watchmaker was really all about watchmaking, and watchmaking was really the organization of distinct tasks such as material procurement, conversion of materials into parts, assembling parts into components, organizing components into a clock, quality control to make sure it was built right, tuning the clock so that it is true, packaging it up to be sold to customers, and so on.

So, a watchmaker was labeled ‘inefficient.’ It became much more productive to downsample the watchmaker into the function of 'watchmaking,' and further downsample that into a sequence of discrete, repeatable processes. This was the Industrial Age: a period where the 'Social Whole' was downsampled into a set of 'Efficient Processes.' We didn't just make more watches; we created a world where the human was only allowed to exist as a low-resolution component of a larger machine.

## Ontological Blinders of the information age
The 20th century saw the rise of the information age. Spearheaded by the likes of Alan Turning, Von Neuman, and Claude Shannon, the balkanized reality of physical processes was 'unified' by mathematical models and algorithmic manipulation. The kinetics in physical reality was recast as information processing, and 'Process Efficiency' become 'algorithmic optimization' in Operations Research.

Between Information Theory and Operations Research, we resurrected Plato's theory of Forms, and the forms were the idealized mathematical models. Any deviation from the model became an 'error' that needed rectification. For instance, the nuances and peculiarities around the problems of routing trains between cities, laying down water and sewer pipes in a neighborhood, and moving data packets around a network were all 'unified' by the same optimization algorithms, and in that process lost those very same nuances and peculiarities. A missed package became an 'error' that needed to be 'corrected' by putting in more 'fault tolerance' into the algorithm; someone calling in sick was also an 'error' that needed 'correctness'. All of this translated to changes in people's lives in subtle, but important ways, but the affected people were none-the-wiser. 

But the insidious turn in this prismatic change was that the model became the reality. All the diversity in the world was now 'errors' in how reality maps to models, _an not the other way around_! So, algorithms that were adapting to the errors were changing people's behavior and their perception of reality to conform to the models, instead of models conforming to reality.

This turn has had an unexpected impact on people's behavior. We now have a society that mistaken the models for reality. The mask has become the face, so to speak. You see it in social media where we constantly mistake someone's 'instagram life' as a true representation of their every day. You see it in the economy, where macro economic metrics such as the GDP is deemed to be the truth of our economic health. In turn, we have no now flattened ourselves to be accounted for by these models. We are figuring out how to 'improve our credit score' as if the credit scoring model were the reality to which we need to conform. 'Pics or it didn't happen', as if algorithmic validation of one's experience is the touchstone of your subjectivity. We have become ontologically blind to anything that exists outside of these models.
