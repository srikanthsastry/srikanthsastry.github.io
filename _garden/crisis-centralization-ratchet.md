# The Crisis Centralization Ratchet

**Tech companies centralize decision-making during crisis and almost never decentralize afterward. The asymmetry is structural: centralizing is fast, decentralizing is slow, and loss aversion fills the gap.**

The ratchet has three compounding layers:

1. **Mechanical asymmetry.** Centralization is a directive: "all decisions go through me now." One meeting. Decentralization requires building judgment, trust, and context at every level of the hierarchy. You can't directive your way into subsidiarity — the transition itself requires the thing you're trying to build. This is structural, not psychological.

2. **Loss aversion.** Even with runway, organizations don't start loosening — because being caught mid-transition when the next crisis arrives feels worse than staying centralized. The perceived risk of unpreparedness dominates the perceived gain of better governance.

3. **Competitive pressure as the only loosening force.** Peacetime loosening isn't voluntary. It's forced by upstarts that are nimble and innovative. Because it's reactive rather than deliberate, it produces shallow structural changes (federated org charts, squad models) rather than deep cultural ones — which is why it snaps back the moment crisis returns.

The three layers compound: the transition is mechanistically slow (1), psychologically avoided (2), and when it happens at all, shallow and reversible (3). If crises come faster than the loosening rate, centralization accumulates.

The existence proofs (Amazon, Netflix, Toyota, mission command) escape all three layers: they invested in culture *before* they needed it (no loss aversion), did it deliberately and deeply (not reactive/shallow), and maintained it long enough for the slow process to take hold.

**Falsifiable prediction:** long peacetime → observable loosening; frequent crises → persistent centralization. The ratchet describes the default trajectory, not the only possible outcome.
