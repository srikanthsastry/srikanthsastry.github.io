# Directive Governance

**Information flows up (compressed and lossy), decisions are made centrally based on that compressed information, directives flow down for execution, and accountability is for compliance with directives rather than for outcomes.** Informally: chain-of-command governance with upward communication.

This is what the tech industry colloquially calls "command and control," but that military term is neutral. The US DoD definition (Joint Publication 1) is simply "the exercise of authority and direction by a properly designated commander over assigned and attached forces." Mission command — which federates decisions to subordinates acting on commander's intent — is a philosophy *within* C2, not its opposite. "Directive governance" is more precise for the specific pattern being critiqued.

Structurally, directive governance in tech is a hybrid with a mechanistic core (Burns & Stalker, 1961) and organic ornamentation. The hierarchy, formal procedures, and top-down decision-making are mechanistic. The "innovation labs," hackathons, and autonomous-team branding are organic decoration. The combination is the cargo cult pattern: adopting the visible rituals of decentralization without changing the underlying decision architecture.

Directive governance depends on three structural preconditions: (1) information is compressible without critical loss, (2) quantitative proxy metrics correlate with reality, and (3) planning and execution are separable. When these hold — manufacturing, logistics, regulated industries — directive governance works. When they fail — as they structurally do in software, where essential complexity is irreducible, metrics measure maintenance not creation, and execution IS decision-making — the governance model produces systematically bad decisions.
